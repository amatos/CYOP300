This portion of the lab had us generate 10 passwords in md5, sha256 and sha512, then check if the hashes could easily be broken by \url{Crackstation.net}. Needless to say, simple 1-word passwords were easily broken; although it would be more accurate to say that \url{Crackstation.net} had the precomputed hashes stored for immediate pattern matching. Also to no surprise, passwords with simple and common substitutions or misspellings were also easily matched, i.e. \enquote{p@\$\$w0rd}, \enquote{p@\$\$\$w0rd}, and other similar variants.

What I did find a little surprising is that hashes for combinations of words were not included in Crackstation.net's archive, such as \enquote{correct horse battery staple}\footnote{See \url{https://xkcd.com/936}}. I would expect that, if no other groups of words, or compound-words are captured in \url{Crackstation.net}'s dictionary, that one, at least would have been.

In the end, the exercise demonstrates two things:
\begin{enumerate}
\item md5 is too vulnerable and should not be used.
\item sha256 and sha512 may be acceptable now, but it will not be with further developments in quantum computing.
\item Salting of passwords, even if only by using a simple salt like \emph{NaCl} significantly improves the odds of a hash surviving a long time.
\end{enumerate}

I believe that current industry passwords of using complex salts and multiple iterations of hashing a hash are the best ways to maintain password viability \emph{for now}.
